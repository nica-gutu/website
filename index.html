About me

I am a computational biologist and physicist with a PhD in Biophysics–Computational Biology and 6 years of research and technical experience in quantitative biology, data science, and computational modeling.

My current postdoctoral research at the Centre for Genomic Regulation (CRG) focuses on cancer genomics, model-ready dataset construction, mechanistic modeling of mutational processes, simulation-based validation, and machine learning for cancer-driver and mutation-rate-modifier discovery. I work primarily in Python to analyze heterogeneous biomedical datasets and develop statistical, nonlinear, and reproducible computational workflows.

During my PhD and doctoral research at Charité and Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, I combined protein- and cell-based experimental data, live-cell assays, time-series analysis, mathematical modeling, and ML-based bioimage analysis to investigate how DNA-damage signaling and circadian regulation shape cell proliferation and drug response across multiple cancer types.

Projects

Cancer Drivers, Mutation-Rate Modifiers & Neutral Genes

Current postdoctoral project · CRG Weghorn Lab

I develop computational approaches to discover novel cancer-driver and mutation-rate-modifier (MRM) genes by distinguishing their expected mutation patterns from neutral/passenger behavior. The project integrates heterogeneous cancer-genomics resources into cancer-type-specific, model-ready datasets and combines mechanistic modelling, controlled simulation and machine learning.

Data curation & feature engineering

Construct biologically informed gene-level features, including important-mutation counts and fractions, mutation-carrier numbers, MMR-associated mutation measures, tumor mutational burden, and transformed expression values. Gene and tumor identifiers are harmonized, positive and neutral reference sets are defined, and gene length and background mutation rate are accounted for to support consistent modeling and classification across cancer types.

Mechanistic modelling

Constrained parametric models describe neutral mutation accumulation, selective driver excess and nonlinear MRM behavior. Competing formulations are evaluated using complementary model-selection and validation criteria, including information criteria, goodness-of-fit and residual diagnostics, statistical inference and cross-validation, alongside checks of identifiability and numerical stability.

Simulation & validation

Driver, MRM and neutral signals are generated from class-specific fitted models while preserving cohort-level mutational properties. These simulated ground-truth datasets are used to quantify detection performance and assess sensitivity to cancer type, mutation burden, carrier counts and filtering thresholds.

Machine learning & reproducibility

Interpretable decision-tree-based classifiers in scikit-learn and PyTorch combine gene-level features to identify driver- and MRM-like behavior and reveal learned decision rules and feature contributions. Reusable command-line workflows make alternative equations, feature definitions, thresholds and simulation settings traceable and reproducible.

DNA Damage Response: Signaling Dynamics and Radiation Response Heterogeneity

I investigated how heterogeneous levels of DNA damage are encoded by the dynamics of the tumor suppressor p53 and its downstream target p21, and how these signals shape proliferation behavior at single-cell and population levels. The work combined long-term live-cell imaging, time-series analysis, quantitative modelling and machine-learning-supported image analysis.

Communications Biology paper

Circadian Rhythm and Cell Growth

I studied how circadian coupling between individual cells regulates tissue growth. The project combined coupled-oscillator theory, long-term single-cell imaging, genetic and non-genetic perturbations, quantitative analysis and ML-based bioimage processing with ilastik. The work demonstrated that the level of circadian coordination modulates cell growth and the timing of cell division.

Nature Physics paper

Drug Response Dynamics in Chronotherapy

This project combined mathematical modelling with controlled in-vitro experiments to identify the cellular factors that shape time-of-day drug sensitivity in human cells. The resulting framework disentangles the influence of the circadian clock, intrinsic drug-response profile, cellular growth and assay constraints on observed treatment-response curves.
